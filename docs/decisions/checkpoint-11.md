# Checkpoint 11 decisions: Ask it the other way round

A control in the level list's toolbar now says which way the next session will ask, and the user can tap it to switch. Left as it ships, a session asks the way it always has: the card's front is the pinyin with the hanzi beneath it, and the back is the English meaning. Turned round, the front is the English meaning alone, with no audio and no speaker to give the answer away, and the back is the pinyin with the hanzi and the speaker. The same words are drawn from the same batches, the same swipe writes the same review row, and the batch moves along the same ladder either way. The setting is remembered between sessions, applies everywhere rather than per level or per word, and cannot change while a session is running.

One line per decision: what was decided, and the citation from the frozen plan, copied rather than paraphrased.

D1. The reverse direction asks for pinyin, never for hanzi. The front is the English meaning and what the user recalls is the spoken form. Cited: route line 11 and route line 4 before it, "a reverse direction that goes from English to pinyin"; the route's Not on the route list, "Reading and writing hanzi, stroke order, handwriting. The user does not want them; the hanzi is on screen only to disambiguate homophones and to drive the speech synthesizer"; checkpoint 1 D6, "never on the back and never tested"; Knell and Chi 2017, "To Delay or Not to Delay: The Timing of Chinese Character Instruction for Secondary Learners", Foreign Language Annals 50(1), where early character instruction produced significantly better reading comprehension and character writing but no significant difference in oral interview or oral fluency scores, so character recall buys a spoken-vocabulary learner nothing measurable.

D2. The reverse direction is off by default and is only ever on because the user turned it on. Cited: Terai, Yamashita and Pasich 2021, "Effects of learning direction in retrieval practice on EFL vocabulary learning", Studies in Second Language Acquisition 43(5), 1116 to 1137, which found a proficiency by direction interaction: for lower-proficiency learners the receptive direction produced better learning, and only for higher-proficiency learners did the productive direction win. Checkpoint 1 D5 already cites this study for choosing the receptive direction, and the review answer of 2026-09-07 recorded the user as a near beginner. Webb 2009, "The effects of receptive and productive learning of word pairs on vocabulary knowledge", is why the direction exists at all: productive practice transferred to both receptive and productive measures, while receptive practice mainly produced receptive gains.

D3. A reverse session grades the same batches on the same ladder. There is no second card, no second interval, and no per-direction memory. Asking a batch in reverse replaces that look's receptive practice with productive practice; it does not add a look, and the batch retires on schedule either way. Cited: checkpoint 2 D1, "no per-word due date, no per-word memory state", from the user's own words on 2026-09-08, "Stop with the due stuff. You are thinking about the process wrong." This departs from the field's practice and the departure is named: SuperMemo's Twenty Rules of Formulating Knowledge, rule 17, holds a swapped-direction item to be a separate item because "active recall does not, however, guarantee passive recognition", and Anki generates two independently scheduled cards from one note for the same reason; Laufer 1998 and Laufer and Paribakht 1998 find receptive and productive knowledge asymmetric and separately decaying. The cost is stated under Failure modes.

D4. The direction is chosen from a control in the level list's toolbar, beside the placement button, which names the direction the next session will ask in. Cited: route line 11 records that no surface was free for this: the level row's leading area starts a session, checkpoint 4 D5 spent its trailing edge on the Browse button, and any control on the session screen is inside a session that has already drawn. The toolbar is the one surface left, and it is the right one: the setting is global by D5, not per level, so a control that lives on a row would say the opposite. Checkpoint 3 D12 already put a global secondary action, the placement re-run, in this same toolbar at `Laoshu/LevelListView.swift`, so this follows a decision the app has made once.

D4a. The control is a text button, not a glyph, and its label is the words "Pinyin first" or "English first", naming which face the next session's cards will open on. Tapping it switches to the other. The label is read from the database, on appear and again after each write, and never set optimistically from the tap, so it always says what the next session will actually do. A write that throws therefore leaves the label where it was, which is the truth, and does nothing else: it does not set the load error that replaces the whole level list with the database error screen, the way a failed placement decline does in `Laoshu/LevelListView.swift`. Losing the home screen because a preference did not save is worse than the preference not saving, and a label that did not move is the honest signal (critique round 2 finding F4). Cited: critique round 1 finding F5. The app has no user-facing name for either direction, and this PRD's own words, receptive and reverse, are the literature's rather than the user's; "Pinyin first" and "English first" name the thing the user will actually see, which is the side the card opens on, and need no explanation. A glyph will not do here because the toolbar's existing control is already a glyph with no visible text and a second one beside it would say nothing about which state it is in; the two states have to be readable at a glance for the screenshot check to mean anything.

D5. The direction is remembered between sessions in a new one-row `preference` table in the writable database, added by migration v5 and seeded with the receptive direction on every existing phone. It is one setting for the whole app, not per level and not per word. Cited: checkpoint 1 D10 keeps every rule a test can check inside `LaoshuKit`, and a direction held in `UserDefaults` is app state `swift test` cannot reach; `LaoshuKit/Sources/LaoshuKit/LaoshuDatabase.swift` already carries the one-row `placement` table added by migration v4 as the shape to follow. Route line 6, "Tune it", will need this table for the rest of its settings; this checkpoint adds it with one column and builds no settings screen.

D6. The direction cannot change while a session is running. A session drawn one way is graded that way to its end. What enforces it is not that the control is out of reach, since checkpoint 4 D13 pushes the session onto the level list's own stack and the back button returns to the toolbar in one tap (critique round 1 finding F7); it is that popping the session destroys it, because checkpoint 1 D22 keeps session state in memory only. Cited: checkpoint 1 D22, which keeps session state in memory with nothing persisted, so a change mid-session would leave the already-swiped cards graded one way and the rest another with nothing recording which was which; checkpoint 2 D3, which makes the first swipe the moment the batch work commits.

D6a. `LaoshuKit` owns the direction, not the app. `Session` captures the direction at the draw and carries it, and `Card` exposes which face asks and which answers, as a two-case value: the Chinese face, which is the pinyin with the hanzi beneath it and the speaker below both, and the meaning face, which is the definition alone. The view renders a face given its kind and never branches on the direction, which is the choice it stops making; where the hanzi and the speaker sit within the Chinese face stays in the view, because that is layout and not a rule (critique round 2 finding F2). Cited: critique round 1 findings F1 and F4. D3 makes the direction change nothing the scheduler does, so if the direction never entered the kit, every automated check in this checkpoint would pass on a build where no card was ever reversed, and D6's rule would have nowhere to live that `swift test` can reach. Checkpoint 1 D10 puts every rule a test can check inside the package; which face asks the question is such a rule, and `Laoshu/CardView.swift` reading it from the card is the same shape as its reading `isFlipped` today.

D7. The reverse card is the two existing faces swapped, not a new card. Front: the English meaning alone, in the size the back uses today. Back: pinyin large, hanzi small and grey beneath it, speaker below both. Cited: `Laoshu/CardView.swift`, which already renders both faces; checkpoint 1 D4 for pinyin with tone marks, D6 for the hanzi beneath the pinyin, D7 for the speaker.

D8. In the reverse direction the audio auto-plays on the reveal, as it does today, because the reveal is now the side carrying the pronunciation; the front carries no speaker and plays nothing, since playing the Chinese before asking for it would answer the question. Cited: checkpoint 1 D7, auto-playing once on flip; `Laoshu/SessionView.swift`, where the first reveal is already what triggers it.

D9. A reverse card whose meaning is shared with another word is asked anyway, with nothing added to the front to narrow it, and the user grades themselves on the word the back reveals. Cited: measured on 2026-09-09 over the shipped catalogue, counting a word as colliding when any other word anywhere in the catalogue carries the same cleaned definition. 274 of 5,400 words collide, in 132 groups; by level, 8.3, 8.0, 4.8, 4.7, 5.4 and 4.2 per cent. The groups are genuine near-synonyms, "to gather; to collect" covering ji, shouji, caiji and souji. This is deliberately not solved: every disambiguator available either leaks the answer, as the hanzi would, or narrows without resolving, as a syllable count would, and checkpoint 1 D6's fix for the same problem in the other direction worked only because the hanzi was free to show. What carries it instead is the user's own grade, per the review answer of 2026-09-08, "If I know it I will say I know it". The cost is named under Failure modes. Measured the same way on the same day, pinyin collisions run 25.3, 25.5, 17.4, 12.6, 12.1 and 9.7 per cent by level, 13.1 per cent over the catalogue, so the meaning is the more discriminating of the two prompts by a wide margin and this direction is the better served one, not the worse. An earlier draft of this decision said the opposite; it was comparing this catalogue-wide figure against the cumulative-subset construction of the existing "Pinyin collisions" table, which is not like for like (critique round 1 finding F6).

D9a. Two words in the same session that share a cleaned definition present a byte-identical front in the reverse direction, and this is allowed: the draw does not filter for definition uniqueness, and a left-swiped card returning three places later is not distinguished from the other word either. The user grades themselves on the word the back reveals, exactly as D9 decides for the cross-word case, and nothing is added to the front. Cited: the same review answer of 2026-09-08, "If I know it I will say I know it", and a measurement taken on 2026-09-09 that D9's catalogue-wide count does not answer, because a session draws from one level only. Counting a word as colliding when another word in the same level shares its cleaned definition, 89 of 5,400 words collide, by level 4.0, 1.0, 0.8, 1.4, 2.1 and 1.3 per cent, against 5.3, 4.0, 6.2, 3.7, 5.0 and 3.2 per cent for pinyin counted the same way. A session draws at most a few dozen of a level's hundreds of words, so two members of a colliding pair meeting inside one session is rarer again. `LaoshuKit/Sources/LaoshuKit/SessionEngine.swift:317` shuffles the level's unseen pool and takes eight with no uniqueness filter, and `:190` requeues a left-swiped card with its flip reset; neither changes (critique round 3 finding F2). This is the only case the receptive direction cannot produce, since a receptive front carries the pinyin and the hanzi, which differ between two words.

D10. `docs/measure.py` gains a "Definition and pinyin collisions by level" table with one row per level 1 to 6 plus an all-levels row, each carrying the word count and, for each of the two prompts, the number and percentage of that level's words whose cleaned definition, or whose toned pinyin, is shared with any other word anywhere in the catalogue, and again counting only words inside the same level, which is the pair of numbers D9a rests on and the catalogue-wide pair cannot give. It is deliberately not the shape of the existing "Pinyin collisions" table, which is three cumulative rows counting collisions only within each subset and cannot produce the per-level figures D9 rests on; that table stays as it is, since checkpoint 1 D6 cites it. The new table is generated from the same TSV and the same `clean()` the rest of `docs/measure.py` uses. D9's numbers were taken from the shipped database, whose definitions `CatalogueBuilder.cleanDefinition` wrote, while the table is generated from the TSV through the Python `clean()`, so the two rules have to be shown to agree. That agreement is one command run by the closure task and recorded in the decision record, not a `swift test` case: no test in `LaoshuKitTests` opens a repository data file, the test target declares no resources, and a test that shelled out to `python3` or depended on the working directory being the repository root would fail whenever `swift test` runs from the package directory, which is where `--package-path` invites it (critique round 2 finding F1). `docs/measure.py` gains a `--dump-definitions` flag printing `word_index` and cleaned definition, one word per line, tab separated, so the command is a plain diff against the same two columns read out of `data/laoshu.sqlite`. `docs/measurements.md` is regenerated to carry it. Cited: D9 rests on a number nothing in the repository records. Checkpoint 1 D3 is the convention it follows, which regenerates `docs/measurements.md` from `docs/measure.py`, and every checkpoint record since carries the `python3 docs/measure.py > /tmp/m.md && diff` line under Checks run, which is what makes the number self-checking rather than remembered. Not checkpoint 1 D14, which is the catalogue rebuild determinism rule and says nothing about measurements (critique round 2 finding F5).

D11. Migration v5 and the read of the preference on the launch path are tested against a database built the way checkpoint 4 shipped it, not one built by the code under test. Cited: checkpoint 4 D10, which widened checkpoint 3 D14 from migrations to any read of state a previously shipped version wrote, after the checkpoint 3 launch crash that was a read and not a migration. The preference is read before the level list draws, which is the same launch path that crashed.

D12. No new package dependency. Cited: checkpoint 2 D23.

D13. The direction applies to the study session and to nothing else. `Laoshu/PlacementCardView.swift` is untouched: a placement card has no back, its answer is taken at face value under checkpoint 3 D1, and it marks words known with no undo under checkpoint 3 D3, so a reversed placement card would show a meaning alone with nothing to reveal and would permanently mark words on a prompt D9 measures as shared 8.3 per cent of the time at level 1. `Laoshu/LevelBrowseView.swift` is untouched too: browse shows both faces at once and asks nothing, so there is no direction for it to have. Cited: critique round 1 finding F2; checkpoint 3 D1 and D3; checkpoint 4 D2.

## Failure modes recorded in the plan

A word retires having been checked in one direction only. The user turns the direction round, answers a batch's second look productively, and the batch retires; nothing ever asked them to recognise those words, or the reverse. Nothing detects it, because D3 keeps one schedule for both directions. This is the priced cost of D3, accepted and carried by nothing. Route line 9's graded level test is not named here: as the route records it, it catches "the words the batches never re-taught", and a word practised three times in one direction was re-taught, so nothing on the route today puts direction coverage on that line (critique round 3 finding F3).

The user is shown "to gather; to collect", says souji, and the back reveals caiji. They knew a correct word and not the asked one. D9 leaves this to their own grade, which means the review row records whatever they decide it should. At level 1 this is roughly one card in twelve.

The user turns the direction round mid-day, having already studied. The batches that took their look this morning took it receptively and will not be asked again in either direction until the ladder brings them back. D6 only stops a change inside a running session; nothing stops one between sessions, and nothing should.

The user swipes a few cards, backs out to the level list, flips the control, and opens the level again. The session they left is gone under checkpoint 1 D22, and its first swipe already advanced the due batches and wrote today's batch under checkpoint 2 D3, so the level now composes an allowance-spent screen and the words that session never reached are not offered again today. This is checkpoint 2's existing behaviour and the direction has nothing to do with it; it is named because flipping the control is a new reason to back out of a session mid-way.

Two words sharing a cleaned definition come up in one reverse session, or one of them comes back requeued. Their fronts are identical and the user cannot tell which word is being asked. They grade themselves on the word the back reveals, per D9a. Nothing detects this and nothing needs to.

The write behind the direction control throws. D4a leaves the label reading the direction still in the database, so nothing lies and the user sees their tap did not take. There is no message and no retry. This is the only new write path this checkpoint adds.

Migration v5 runs against a phone holding checkpoint 4's database and fails, or seeds the wrong default, and the app either will not launch or quietly starts asking the harder direction to a near beginner. D11 is the rule that must catch this before it ships, and it is the fourth checkpoint in a row where this is the failure with the most to lose.

The toolbar gains a second control and the level list's header starts to look like a settings screen. Route line 6 is the settings screen and will move this control into it; until then the toolbar carries two.

## Defects

Found during verification, not fixed here. Driving the built card in the simulator turned up something no test covers: 73 of the 5,400 shipped definitions contain hanzi, and 59 of them contain a character from the word's own hanzi, 7 of those at level 1. `日` reads "abbr. for 日本[Ri4 ben3], Japan; sun" and `好玩儿` reads "erhua variant of 好玩[hao3 wan2]". In the receptive direction that is noise on the back of the card. In the reverse direction the definition is the prompt, so those 59 cards print their own answer.

The plan was frozen before this was found. Route line 8 is where the unclear definitions get rewritten, and the route now carries this measurement, 73 definitions containing hanzi, 59 of them containing the word's own hanzi, 7 of those at level 1, against that line. Not fixed here and the catalogue is not changed here: this task runs the checkpoint's checks and records what they show, and rewriting definitions is out of its scope.
