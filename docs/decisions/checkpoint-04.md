# Checkpoint 4 decisions: Look without being tested

One line per decision: what was decided, and the citation from the frozen plan, copied rather than paraphrased.

D1. The two ways to study are the graded test the app already runs and the new browse pass. Nothing about the existing session changes: the draw of checkpoint 2 D5, the swipe gate of checkpoint 1 D26, the requeue of checkpoint 1 D20 and the summary of checkpoint 2 D24 all stand, untouched. Cited: route line 4, "a browse mode that only shows, a graded self test that asks". The graded self test is `Laoshu/SessionView.swift` as it stands, and this checkpoint names it rather than building it. It is not the level test of route line 9, "a graded test over the whole level, passed at a set percentage, that declares the level done and catches the words the batches never re-taught", which is a different thing on its own line: level-wide rather than a day's cards, scored against a pass mark, reached from the finished-level screen of checkpoint 2 D22, and building the whole of it here would leave line 9 with nothing in it. Line 4's contrast is between a mode that only shows and one that asks, and the session that already ships is the one that asks. Recorded as answered under Q4.

D2. Browsing writes nothing. No review row, no batch, no ladder advance, no allowance spent, no placement change. A browse pass is invisible to every query the scheduler makes. Cited: Karpicke and Roediger 2008, "The Critical Importance of Retrieval for Learning", Science 319(5865), where learners who kept restudying pairs they could already recall scored 33 to 36 per cent at one week against 80 per cent for those who kept retrieving them, at equal trial counts, d = 4.03 with no overlap between distributions. Restudy that counted as study would let the user replace the practice that works with the practice that does not, and the batch model of checkpoint 2 D1 would record it as a look that happened.

D3. Browse shows only words the user has already met in that level: every word belonging to any batch of that level, retired batches included. Words never introduced are not shown. Cited: checkpoint 2 D14, "a word counts as introduced when it belongs to any batch", which is the app's existing definition of met and needs no new state to read; Karpicke and Roediger 2008 as cited in D2, whose result is specifically about study-only exposure to material the retrieval loop should own instead. A preview of unseen words is answered under Q2. Batch membership is kept rather than narrowed to words carrying a `review` row, even though checkpoint 2 D13 puts every word a session drew into the batch and not only the ones reached: a review row is the only other predicate available, and the words a placement test marked as known carry none, since `PlacementSession.finish` writes retired batches through `BatchStore.createRetiredBatch` and no review rows at all. Narrowing would drop exactly the list the user is most likely to open browse to read. What batch membership admits instead is named under Failure modes and checked.

D4. Browse is reached from the level list row and does not take the tap that starts a session. That tap keeps the whole of the row except the trailing glyph D5 carves out of it: the level's name and its counts, which is everything a person aims at today. Cited: checkpoint 2 D5 makes the session the daily loop and this checkpoint's Slice keeps it one tap from the list; Karpicke and Roediger 2008 as cited in D2, which makes putting browse on the daily path a design that competes with the practice that works.

D5. The browse control is a visible button at the trailing edge of every level row, beside the counts, not a swipe action and not a gesture. The row stops being one `NavigationLink` and becomes two tap targets in an `HStack`: the leading area, carrying the level name and its counts, opens the session, and the trailing glyph opens browse. Both are borderless buttons, which is what makes two of them individually tappable inside a `List` row. The row loses the chevron that `NavigationLink` draws for it. Cited: critique round 3 finding F1. Browse is the whole of this checkpoint and this control is its only door, so a control with no affordance is the one thing that can make the checkpoint ship and still not reach the user, and no automated check can catch it. The app already answered this question once the same way: checkpoint 3 D12 put the re-run of the placement test in a visible toolbar control at `Laoshu/LevelListView.swift` line 46, not behind a gesture. A toolbar control cannot serve here because browse needs a level and the toolbar belongs to the list rather than to a row. Recorded as answered under Q1, where the swipe action this replaces is kept with the reason it was rejected.

D6. Browse is a SwiftUI `List`, not a `ScrollView` of a `VStack`. Cited: `Laoshu/LevelListView.swift` lines 20 to 37, the app's existing `List`, which is the container this copies; `List` realizes rows lazily, and checkpoint 1 D3 puts 1,800 words in level 6, so a container that builds every row and every speaker button at once is the wrong one at the top of that level. `Laoshu/PlacementResultView.swift` is the pattern for what a row holds, pinyin then meaning, and not for the container around it: that screen is a `ScrollView` wrapping a `VStack` and a `ForEach`, which is right for at most twenty-five words and wrong here.

D7. Browse lists the most recently met words first: by the batch's `created_on` descending, then by the batch's `id` descending, then by `word_index` ascending within a batch. Cited: checkpoint 2 D10 stores dates rather than timestamps, so `created_on` alone cannot order two batches of one level created on the same day, which is the ordinary case and not an edge: checkpoint 2 D19's eight-more button writes a second batch dated the same day with no limit on presses, and checkpoint 3 D5 writes a retired placement batch dated today. `LaoshuKit/Sources/LaoshuKit/LaoshuDatabase.swift` lines 84 to 92 declare `id INTEGER PRIMARY KEY AUTOINCREMENT`, which is the only monotonic tiebreak the schema offers and orders same-day batches by the order they were written. Checkpoint 1 D17 fixes `word_index` order within a level. The user's reason for opening browse is to look back at what was just learned, which is the newest batch.

D8. A level with no batches shows a browse screen that says so in a sentence, and never an empty list. Cited: checkpoint 2 D22, which established that this app names an empty state rather than rendering one, and checkpoint 3 D17a, which did the same for a placement test that marked nothing.

D9. The browse screen owns one `SpeechSpeaker` for the whole list, and tapping a second row's speaker interrupts the first rather than overlapping it. Cited: `Laoshu/SpeechSpeaker.swift` lines 15 to 21, where `speak` calls `synthesizer.stopSpeaking(at: .immediate)` before every utterance, so one shared instance interrupts cleanly and a per-row instance would give one synthesizer per word and overlapping speech; It is held in `@State`, not as a plain stored property: `Laoshu/SessionView.swift` line 14 declares `private let speaker = SpeechSpeaker()` on a `View` struct, whose initialiser runs again every time the parent rebuilds the destination, and two instances mean two `AVSpeechSynthesizer`s, each of whose `stopSpeaking(at: .immediate)` reaches only its own. That line is the pattern for one speaker per screen and is not the pattern for making it survive a rebuild. On a phone with no Mandarin voice the browse row follows the card front's existing rule rather than inventing one: `Laoshu/SpeechSpeaker.swift` lines 9 to 13 set `voiceAvailable` false and make `speak` a no-op, and `Laoshu/CardView.swift` lines 35 to 40 draw the glyph struck through, greyed and disabled, which is what the row does too. A speaker that looks live and does nothing is the defect this prevents. Leaving the browse screen stops whatever is speaking: the screen's speaker is destroyed with its state on the pop, and `stopSpeaking(at: .immediate)` reaches only its own synthesizer, so an utterance left running would play over the session's auto-play on the first flip, which is the two-voices outcome one screen removed.

D10. The browse query is tested against a database built the way checkpoint 2 shipped it, with real batch and batch_word rows in it, not one built by the code under this checkpoint. This widens checkpoint 3 D14, which covered migrations only, to cover any read of state a previously shipped version wrote. Cited: the second launch crash of checkpoint 3, recorded at `docs/decisions/checkpoint-03.md`, which was a read and not a migration: a phone seeded by checkpoint 3 D13a carried a null recommended level that a non-optional decode trapped on, and both the suite and the compile gate stayed green because every test database had been built by the current code. Browse reads nothing but rows that checkpoint 2 and checkpoint 3 wrote.

D11. This checkpoint changes no schema, adds no migration and adds no package dependency. Browse reads `batch`, `batch_word` and the attached catalogue and writes nothing. Cited: D2 for the writes; checkpoint 2 D11 for the tables browse reads; checkpoint 2 D23 for the dependency line.

D12. The reverse direction, from English to pinyin, is not in this checkpoint. It goes to the route as line 11, "Ask it the other way round", with the two questions the critic raised against it recorded there: where the direction control lives, and what the reverse card's front carries when two words share a cleaned definition. Cited: critique round 1 finding F5, which counted the two halves at six pull requests against the split rule's ceiling of five, and named the boundary they already have: they share no decision but the closure task. Checkpoint 2's own record says a sixth pull request was cut once already for the same reason, at `docs/decisions/checkpoint-02.md`, "the sixth pull request critique round 1 cut; the split is rebalanced".

D13. The browse screen is pushed onto the level list's own navigation stack, not presented as a sheet or a cover. The stack's path stops being `[Int]` and becomes an array of a two-case destination, one case carrying the level to study and one carrying the level to browse, and the placement push of checkpoint 3 D11b appends the study case. Cited: `Laoshu/LevelListView.swift` line 11 types `path` as `[Int]` and lines 40 to 42 map `Int` to `SessionView`, so `Int` is already spoken for and a second destination cannot be pushed without a type that distinguishes them; line 63 onward puts `.onAppear` on the content inside the stack, which checkpoint 2 D26 requires so the waiting counts recompute every time the list comes back to the top, and only a push comes back that way. A sheet would leave the counts as they were and make the Slice's promise, that the waiting count is the same after a browse as before it, true for the wrong reason: not recomputed rather than recomputed and unchanged. Checkpoint 3 D11b decided the same question for the placement test and is the shape this follows; its `path.append(recommendedLevel)` becomes an append of the study case.

D14. A browse row carries the pinyin and the hanzi on the leading side, the hanzi small and grey directly beneath the pinyin, and the meaning trailing, wrapping to as many lines as it needs; the speaker sits at the trailing edge of the row. Cited: checkpoint 1 D6, "Keep it all on the front small under the pinyin", which fixes the hanzi's position relative to the pinyin wherever both are shown; checkpoint 1 D7 for the speaker glyph; `Laoshu/PlacementResultView.swift` lines 35 to 42 for pinyin then meaning on one baseline-aligned row, which is the app's existing word row and carries neither the hanzi nor a speaker, so this decision says where those two go rather than leaving it to the builder. `Laoshu/CardView.swift` lines 26 to 41 is the only place in the app carrying all four, and it stacks them inside a 260-point card, which is not a row.

D15. The closure task amends `docs/decisions/checkpoint-01.md`, whose D5 states "The reverse direction is checkpoint 4", to record that the reverse direction left this checkpoint under D12 and is route line 11; writes `docs/decisions/checkpoint-04.md` in the shape of the existing records, and rewrites the README's line at `README.md` lines 5 to 11, "Pick an HSK level, swipe through its words", which describes one way to study in a checkpoint named for two. ## Failure modes recorded in the plan

The user browses instead of studying, and no batch ever advances, so the app quietly stops teaching while appearing to be in use. D2 is what makes this possible and there is no counter-measure here: browse is deliberately free. What the app does is refuse to count it, so the level list's waiting counts keep telling the truth about what has not been studied.

Browse is opened at the top of level 6 after long use and the list holds many hundreds of rows. D6 makes it a `List`, so rows and their speaker buttons are built as they are scrolled to. The count is bounded at 1,800 by checkpoint 1 D3, and there is no paging beyond what the container does.

The user taps one row's speaker while another is still speaking. D9 makes the second tap interrupt the first, so the outcome is one voice, cut off mid-word. Overlapping speech is what happens if a builder gives each row its own speaker, and a phone step names this.

The user starts a word speaking in browse, leaves immediately, and taps into a session. D9 stops the utterance on the way out, so the session's own auto-play on the first flip is the only voice. Without that rule the two synthesizers cannot reach each other and both play.

A word appears in two batches of the same level, which nothing in the schema forbids, and browse lists it twice. Checkpoint 2 D14 makes batch membership the definition of introduced and the draw excludes introduced words, so no ordinary path writes a word into a second batch; a duplicated row would be a symptom of a defect elsewhere rather than a browse defect, and browse does not hide it.

A session is abandoned after its first swipe. Checkpoint 2 D13 writes all eight drawn words into the batch at that swipe, so up to seven of them were never shown, and D3 lists them in browse as words already met. The user gets a study-only look at words the retrieval loop has not asked for yet, which is the exposure Q2 refuses for unseen words. It is bounded and it is temporary: it is at most one session's unreached words, they are still live on the ladder, and they come back the next day whether or not they were browsed. Nothing on the screen tells them apart from the word that was actually swiped.

Browse is opened on a level whose only batch is the retired placement batch of checkpoint 3 D5. Those words were marked known and never taught, and D3 lists them, because they are words the user said they know and browse is where they would look for them. The screen does not distinguish them from words that were studied.

## The research the browse rules rest on

Restudying material already recalled measures far below retrieving it: Karpicke and Roediger 2008, "The Critical Importance of Retrieval for Learning", Science 319(5865), found that learners who kept restudying pairs they could already recall scored 33 to 36 per cent at one week against 80 per cent for those who kept retrieving them, at equal trial counts, d = 4.03 with no overlap between distributions. This is why browsing writes nothing, per D2, and why it never previews words the user has not yet met, per D3.

## What browse deliberately shows that the user has not seen

A session abandoned after one swipe writes all eight drawn words into the batch at that swipe, per checkpoint 2 D13, so up to seven of them were never actually shown to the user. D3 lists all of them in browse regardless, because batch membership rather than a review row is what checkpoint 2 D14 calls introduced, and narrowing to words carrying a review row would drop exactly the list the user is most likely to open browse to read. This is named in Failure modes as bounded and temporary: at most one session's unreached words, still live on the ladder, returning the next day whether or not they were browsed.
